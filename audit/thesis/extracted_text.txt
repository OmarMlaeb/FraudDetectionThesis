Detecting Financial Fraud Using Graph Neural

Omar Malaeb
ABSTRACT
Detecting financial fraud in digital transactions and cryptocurrencies depends on
analyzing the usefulness of the information gained from the relationships between
the transactions. In this thesis, we conduct a comparative benchmarking of three
neural networks not based on graphs (Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Long Short-
Term Memory (LSTM), and Transformer) along with three graph neural networks
(Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), GraphSAGE, and Graph Attention Network
(GAT)).
Each transaction is depicted by a node. In IEEE-CIS, five graphs are created, one
graph that merges matches over selected attributes, and four others according to
joint card signature, joint address signature, common email domains, and common
identifiers in 24 hours. The main, card, address, and email-domain constructions
connect consecutive transactions in chronological order within eligible matching
groups. The identifier-time-window construction connects all eligible pairs sharing
vi

=== PDF PAGE 8 ===
a nonmissing identifier value within 24 hours. Excessively large groups are not used
for creating edges. Elliptic provides transaction edges that describe Bitcoin flow.
The GNNs leverage the edges to combine information from adjacent nodes, while
non-graph baselines do not utilize explicit graph adjacency. Alternative IEEE-CIS
graphs are considered to see how provided relationships influence each GNN.
Performance is evaluated based on recall, precision, F1-measure, PR-AUC, ROC-
AUC, and confusion matrices where PR-AUC and F1-measure are the key perfor-
mance indicators. Key comparison and graph generation experiments employ ten
paired random seeds with a fixed time-based train-validation-test split. Paired dif-
ferences are analyzed with the use of the unadjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank test.
For IEEE-CIS, the highest average PR-AUC is obtained by MLP, and the highest
average F1 score is obtained by Transformer. For IEEE-CIS, Email-Domain Links
achieve the highest average PR-AUC for GraphSAGE, 24-Hour ID Graph achieves
the highest average PR-AUC for GCN, and Main Graph achieves the highest average
PR-AUC for GAT. There is no statistically significant difference in PR-AUC between
MLP and GraphSAGE with Email-Domain Links on the unadjusted test; this does
not show equivalence. For Elliptic, the highest average PR-AUC and F1 score are
obtained by Transformer.
The results have not provided any conclusive evidence regarding the dominance of
GNNs in comparison to other non-graph neural network models. Graph construction
influences architecture in different ways, and increased connectivity and coverage of
fraud nodes do not guarantee improved performance in fraud detection.
Keywords: Financial Fraud Detection, Graph Neural Networks, Deep Learning,

Chapter One
Introduction
1.1 Overview and Motivation
One of the most critical problems facing today’s financial systems, digital payment
systems, and cryptocurrency systems is financial frauds. Due to the huge opportu-
nities for frauds in today’s digital banking, mobile payments, and online commerce,
the total losses due to payment frauds alone run into billions of dollars worldwide
every year. Although conventional rule-based systems and statistical methods were
quite successful in detecting frauds in the past, today they are facing difficulties in
identifying complex frauds that exploit complex relationship patterns among differ-
ent entities, accounts, and transactions.
Properties of transactions, sequences of transactions, and graph relationships can
all be considered as distinct representations for fraud detection. By using the graph
method, it is possible to have the model take into account the information contained
1

=== PDF PAGE 17 ===
within related transactions; however, the value of this relationship depends on its
definition as well as the information in the existing features.
In the case of this thesis work, nodes of each graph represent individual transac-
tions. For IEEE-CIS, edges are generated based on the matching and temporal
rules using shared attributes of transactions. Main edge generation is based on the
matching of selected card, address, email-domain, device, and identity attributes.
Four additional edge generation methods consider the joint card signatures, joint ad-
dress signatures, common email-domains, and common identifiers within 24 hours
respectively. These edges are attributed based associations, and do not necessarily
represent payments between transactions nor connections between same customers.
In the case of Elliptic, dataset provides edges representing the Bitcoin flows between
transactions. Specific edge generation methods are explained in Section 3.4.
For GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT methods, the adjacency of generated graph is
used for aggregating the features of neighboring transactions along with the features
of each transaction itself. For MLP, LSTM, and Transformer baselines, their corre-
sponding representations based on features or sequences are used without transaction-
graph adjacency. Applications of graph-based models include the credit card fraud
detection [1], and illicit Bitcoin transactions classification [2, 3].
Previous research provides results on the success of specific graph-based architec-
tures and non-graph architectures under their own experimental frameworks. Such
research inspires comparison despite not showing that the use of graph-based mod-
els will be better in all datasets. The pertinent issue lies in assessing the impact of
the available transaction relationship and comparing its performance against other
2

=== PDF PAGE 18 ===
non-graph approaches.
This thesis explores this issue through comparing GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT with
MLP, LSTM, and Transformer on IEEE-CIS and Elliptic datasets. The models will
be compared based on predefined preprocessing methods, chronological splits of
the datasets, and identical measures of performance. In addition, the experiments
on the IEEE-CIS dataset differ in the graph construction criteria and assessment of
structure. The goal here is to assess the conditions where the GNNs are competitive,
can gain from other relationship types, or fail to beat non-graph baselines.
1.2 The Shift Toward Relational Learning in Fi-
nance
Over the past 20 years, many paradigm shifts have been noticed in the use of ma-
chine learning for financial fraud detection. In addition, earlier techniques employed
statistical anomaly detection and rule-based systems, which were not very effective
in adapting to changing fraud schemes. More complex pattern recognition was made
possible by the introduction of supervised machine learning techniques, such as ran-
dom forests, decision trees, and support vector machines, but these techniques were
still fundamentally limited by their dependence on manually created features and
independent transaction assumptions.
Fraud detection now has strong representation learning skills because of the deep
learning revolution. MLPs provide a baseline for learning non-linear relationships in
tabular features [4]. In fraud detection, LSTMs have been used to model sequential
3

=== PDF PAGE 19 ===
information in transaction histories [5]. More recently, Transformer architectures
have also been adapted to tabular data, using self-attention to model interactions
between feature representations [4]. Table comparisons of data have studied deep
learning models alongside gradient boosted decision trees, highlighting the impor-
tance of consistency in training methodologies and hyperparameters tuning practices
[4].
The difference is in the types of dependencies that these architectures capture. While
the MLP works on the provided feature vector, the sequence models have the ca-
pability of working with dependencies within the input sequences. Nevertheless,
these two architectures do not provide an automatic method of representing the re-
lationship between the transactions. The graph model provides a way of providing
this kind of representation. Whether this extra representation will be helpful for
detecting fraud will depend on the type of relationships retained, architecture used,
and the features provided.
Graph Neural Networks directly work with graph data, and offer a way to integrate
information about a node and its neighboring nodes. Moreover, Graph Convolu-
tional Networks, which extend convolutional operations to irregular graph struc-
tures using spectral graph theory, were first introduced in the significant study
of Kipf and Welling [6]. Thus, this allows neural networks to learn representa-
tions that encode both structural context and node attributes by aggregating data
from a node’s local neighborhood. In addition, this framework was expanded by
Hamilton et al.[7] with GraphSAGE, which introduced inductive learning capabil-
ities through neighborhood sampling and aggregation functions that allow gener-
alization to previously unseen nodes—a significant requirement for fraud detection
4

=== PDF PAGE 20 ===
systems that have to constantly adapt to new accounts and transaction patterns.
Furthermore, GNNs have shown great promise when used for fraud detection. Us-
ing real-world card transaction data, Cheng et al. [8] created a spatial-temporal
attention GNN (STAGN) that outperforms state-of-the-art baselines by combining
graph structure with temporal and location-based features. Moreover, Pareja et
al. evaluated EvolveGCN and static graph-based baselines on the Elliptic dataset,
examining illicit Bitcoin transaction classification in a temporal setting [2].
Recent research (2023–2026) has further refined GNN-based fraud detection through
several key innovations. Temporal heterogeneous graph contrastive learning ap-
proaches have been developed to capture both relational structure and temporal
dynamics in credit card transactions [9]. Hybrid architectures have also been intro-
duced. For instance, STA-GT uses temporal encoding, heterogeneous graph model-
ing, and Transformer modules for capturing local and global transactions informa-
tion [10]. Graph-aware sampling [11], cost-sensitive learning [12], rule augmentation
[13], and dual-view representation learning [14] are some of the advanced techniques
that have been suggested as a way of handling the problem of extreme class im-
balance, which is a fundamental problem since fraudulent transactions represent a
small minority of the total transactions, with the proportion varying across datasets.
The performance of graph neural networks versus non-graph neural networks thus
becomes an empirical issue for a specific evaluation environment. This thesis will test
the performance of both graph and non-graph neural models in the case of IEEE-CIS
and Elliptic data sets, but also look at the effect of other ways of constructing graphs
from the IEEE-CIS data set on the performance of GNNs. The study will discuss
both successes and challenges, drawing connections between the results and the
5

=== PDF PAGE 21 ===
properties of graph connectivity and isolation, as well as the relationships between
classes.
1.3 Problem Statement
Financial fraud detection models are challenged by a fundamental modeling issue
since most fraud models exhibit hidden relationship structures that are not reflected
in individual transaction-based feature models. Moreover, repetitive transactions
between two or more entities, device IDs for different accounts, synchronized fraud-
ulent behavior, and sophisticated money laundering schemes are all network-based
models that are difficult to identify using traditional deep learning models that
operate on individual transactions.
Research Problem:How can relational structures embedded within large-scale fi-
nancial transaction data be effectively leveraged to improve financial fraud detection
beyond conventional transaction-level deep learning models?
This problem can arise in various ways:
Modeling Dimension:Without specific tools to handle multi-hop relational de-
pendencies, traditional deep learning architectures such as MLPs, LSTMs, and
Transformers rely on patterns that are sequential or tabular. While these mod-
els have the ability to learn complex patterns and feature interactions over time,
they cannot directly incorporate the graph structure that is commonly seen in fraud
schemes. On the other hand, despite their design to handle graph-structured data
naturally, GNN models may suffer from problems of high class imbalance, high
6

=== PDF PAGE 22 ===
scalability, and temporal dependencies.
Data Dimension:As a point of fact, the structural properties of financial trans-
action data can differ significantly. For example, the Elliptic Bitcoin dataset has
an explicit structure, where the data can be treated as a network. On the other
hand, the IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection dataset is structured but has some hidden re-
lationship information due to common attribute values (device IDs, email domains,
IP addresses), which can be used to construct an implicit network. The best ap-
proach may be highly dependent on the nature of the fraud signals: relationship vs.
feature-based.
Evaluation Dimension:Fraud detection typically involves substantial class im-
balance, where fraudulent transactions represent a small minority of the total trans-
actions. Recall (sensitivity to fraud), F1-score (harmonic mean of precision and
recall), and Precision-recall AUC (area under precision-recall curve) are metrics
that emphasize the importance of detecting minority classes, as accuracy measures
are misleading in this context. Along with this, it is critical to have evaluation
parameters to assess GNN and DL methods in an unbiased manner.
Operational Dimension:For real-world fraud detection systems, millions of
transactions need to be processed in real-time, and thus efficient inference and up-
dating are necessary. In fact, building graphs from tabular data involves certain
computational costs, and message passing in GNNs can also be computationally ex-
pensive for large graphs. Furthermore, for the applicability of GNN-based methods,
in addition to detection performance, certain computational viability and scalability
are also necessary.
7

=== PDF PAGE 23 ===
In this thesis, I investigate the factors under which graph modeling is effective for
obtaining good prediction performance in detecting financial fraud and when non-
graph approaches remain more effective. I benchmark existing neural networks on
IEEE-CIS and Elliptic datasets and assess the impact of different graphs that can be
constructed from the IEEE-CIS dataset on the performance of GNNs. This includes
an assessment of the graph’s connectivity, isolation, number of connected compo-
nents, density, and connectivity per each class, along with the detection measures in
order to help understand the observed differences. The relationships are found for
the settings studied; causality between the graph characteristics and performance is
not established.
1.4 Research Questions
This thesis is motivated by the following three main research questions, which focus
on the fundamental modeling, performance, and methodological issues of compara-
tive fraud detection:
RQ1: Can Graph Neural Networks (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT) outper-
form advanced deep learning architectures (MLP, LSTM, Transformer-
based models) by learning network-level dependencies within financial
transaction data?
The current research aims to evaluate if there is an evident advantage of relational
learning in detecting financial fraud. This is achieved by comparing different GNN
models and deep learning baseline models using similar preprocessing, split of data,
8

=== PDF PAGE 24 ===
handling of class imbalance, training methods, and evaluation metrics. The compar-
ison concentrates on overall prediction accuracy of the models on both the IEEE-CIS
and Elliptic datasets in terms of Recall, F1-score, PR-AUC, and ROC-AUC.
RQ2: Under which dataset and graph-construction conditions do the
evaluated GNNs provide useful fraud detection performance, and how
are their performance differences associated with graph connectivity, iso-
lation, and class-specific relationships?
The research explores the posed question through performing comparative analysis
using non-graph models on the IEEE-CIS and Elliptic datasets, and comparing
five different graph structures for each GNN architecture. There are two types of
gain observed: firstly, the gain in comparison with an alternative graph structure
within the same GNN architecture, and secondly, the gain in comparison with a
non-graph baseline model. The performance indicators used include the PR-AUC,
F1-score, recall, and false positives. All of these are considered in relation to the
graph properties.
RQ3: Which modeling paradigm achieves superior fraud detection per-
formance, particularly in terms of Recall, F1-score, and PR-AUC under
highly imbalanced fraud detection conditions?
The current research evaluates the model performance with respect to the class
imbalance inherent in both datasets. All tested models are trained using positive-
class weights, while the performance metrics include recall, precision, F1 score, PR
AUC, and ROC AUC. The evaluation of the tradeoff between the detection of fraud
and restriction of false positives is considered. Comparison with other methods for
9

=== PDF PAGE 25 ===
addressing class imbalance is not provided.
The research questions are operationalized through controlled experiments on the
IEEE-CIS and Elliptic datasets, where the data splits and evaluation protocols are
consistent and the metrics are comprehensively reported to allow direct comparison
across the model families.
1.5 Objectives and Contributions
The thesis provides a comparative benchmarking analysis between well-known GNNs
and other non-GNN deep learning models for fraud detection. The contribution of
the thesis includes the analysis of the predictive performance on the IEEE-CIS and
Elliptic datasets, the analysis of the variability among different runs of the model,
and the analysis of the impact of using different constructions of the graphs in
GNNs. Moreover, the experiments conducted with the IEEE-CIS dataset consider
relationships between parties through card data, addresses, email domains, and IDs.
The first objective is to explain the circumstances where graph models perform com-
petitively, show improvement using a different graph approach, or perform poorly
compared to their counterparts without graphs. Model ranking serves as the first
source of information in the explanation. Consequently, the IEEE-CIS construction
experiment examines if changes in the transaction relations bring any positive per-
formance across various GNNs. Connectivity statistics related to the structure and
classes help in the interpretation of the results, whereas the Elliptic database serves
as the second source of context. Dataset differences do not serve as a stand-alone
10

=== PDF PAGE 26 ===
assessment of the graph.
This is achieved through:
Contribution 1: Unified Comparative Evaluation Framework
This paper introduces a comprehensive experimental setting for the comparison
of six neural networks in terms of their ability to detect fraud in finances. The
neural networks that will be assessed include three non-graph deep neural networks
(Multi-Layer Perceptron, LSTM, Transformer), and three graph neural networks
(GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT).
The comparison process takes into account uniform preprocessing of the data set,
chronologically split train/validate/test samples, and techniques for dealing with
class imbalance using the weighted binary cross-entropy. However, for model check-
pointing and early stopping, we need validation PR-AUC. Finally, the classification
threshold is selected to maximize validation F1-score. We use this threshold with-
out making any changes to our test data.Contribution 2: Graph Construction
and Graph Structure Analysis
With respect to the IEEE-CIS framework, the investigation evaluates a primary
transaction graph as well as four alternative graphs constructed using card details,
addresses, email domains, and common identifiers within a 24-hour interval. GCN,
GraphSAGE, and GAT models are tested on these graphs to see how the choice of
the relational data structure affects the performance of prediction.
The measures used include PR-AUC, F1-score, recall, false positives, and graph
theory measures such as average degree, the number of connected components, den-
sity, the ratio of isolated nodes, and connectivity for each class. This comparison is
11

=== PDF PAGE 27 ===
aimed at discovering any relationships between the construction of graphs and model
performance without assuming that better connectivity will necessarily improve the
result.
The models are also tested on the native transaction graph of Elliptic, and the sam-
pled complement experiment is conducted where the edges of the original graph are
substituted by sampled non-edges. In this case, cross-dataset comparisons provide
supplementary findings but fail to distinguish between graph structure and differ-
ences in features and class distribution.
Contribution 3: Repeated-Run Statistical Evaluation
For the increase of accuracy during the comparison experiment, ten paired random
seeds are used for the primary models. This helps to avoid coming to conclusions
based on a single run of training since the performance of a neural network can be
affected by random initialization, optimization, and threshold selection processes.
In order to make a statistical comparison of the performance of the models in pairs,
the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is used in this thesis. This paired non-parametric test
is used for the determination of statistical significance of the difference in perfor-
mance of the models being compared.
Contribution 4: Reproducible Implementation and Open-Source Release
To support reproducibility, the implementation developed for this thesis is orga-
nized and released as a project repository. The repository includes the preprocess-
ing scripts, graph construction scripts, model implementations, experiment runners,
metric reporting, ranking scripts, and Wilcoxon statistical testing code used in the
experiments.

Collectively, these contributions provide an empirical evaluation of when graph-
based models are useful for financial fraud detection and when feature-based or
sequence-based deep learning models remain more effective. The thesis shows that
graph structure can provide useful information, especially in graph-native data, but
that graph-based learning does not automatically guarantee superior fraud detection
performance.
1.6 Significance of the Study
This research addresses an important theoretical, methodological, and practical gap
with respect to graph neural networks, deep learning, and financial fraud detection.
Theoretical Significance
In this thesis, the comparative results between graph-based, feature-based, and
sequence-based approaches for detecting fraud are empirically presented. The use of
alternative IEEE-CIS networks allows us to study how the predictive power changes
depending on the connections used by each GNN model. The obtained results refer
to the studied datasets and experimental settings; the adversarial robustness is not
considered.
13

=== PDF PAGE 29 ===
The study also makes a contribution in the area of the interplay between the struc-
ture of the graph and the problem of class imbalance. Indeed, recent theoretical
work has demonstrated that the effectiveness of GNNs may be adversely impacted
in the case of a class imbalance problem due to the dynamics of the message pass-
ing mechanism [14], [15]. In this thesis, this issue is examined empirically through
the use of class-weighted loss, threshold calibration, and evaluation metrics that are
suitable for imbalanced fraud detection, including recall, F1-score, and PR-AUC.
Methodological Significance
Methodologically, this research establishes standardized evaluation protocols for
comparing graph-based and feature-based fraud detection models. The lack of con-
sistent benchmarking has hindered progress in the field, with different studies using
different datasets, evaluation metrics, preprocessing procedures, and baseline im-
plementations, making it difficult to draw general conclusions about relative model
performance [3]. By implementing a comprehensive evaluation framework with con-
trolled experimental conditions, this thesis provides a methodological template for
future comparative studies.
The methodology developed in the process of building the graph, based on the tabu-
lar transaction data, is a significant methodological achievement. Although datasets
like Elliptic have a clear graph structure, the majority of the real-world fraud detec-
tion systems rely on tabular transaction data. The present thesis critically assesses
the methodologies of building a significant graph based on the tabular data, which
is applicable in the real world in the process of implementing GNN in the fraud
detection systems based on the tabular data.

Practical Significance
The detection of financial fraud involves the need for detecting fraud and misclas-
sifying good cases as fraudulent. An improvement in the detection of fraud may
be useful as it prevents fraud from going undetected while producing minimal false
positive classifications. The current thesis provides empirical proof that would help
in making decisions regarding model selection and graph construction.
The results have practical implications for choosing models in fraud detection sys-
tems as well. On one hand, financial organizations often work with tabular trans-
action data; on the other hand, cryptocurrency and anti-money laundering tasks
may involve naturally graph-structured transaction networks. Thus, analyzing both
types of data helps clarify when graph-based models are appropriate and when
feature-based or sequence-based deep learning models may be more effective.
Furthermore, the results show that it is crucial to evaluate models used in the process
of fraud detection by metrics like recall, precision, F1-score, PR-AUC, ROC-AUC,
and confusion matrices. In the case of real-world fraud detection systems, if a model
has high recall but generates a large number of false positives, then this model will
hardly be applicable due to an increased number of false alarms.
Broader Impact
Outside the application in the detection of financial fraud, this research has signifi-
cant implications in the anomaly detection of network systems. The methodologies
and the research are relevant and applicable in other related domains, such as cy-
bersecurity (identifying malicious actors in a computer network), healthcare fraud
(identifying fraudulent insurance claims through a network of providers), and social

Chapter Two
Related Work
2.1 Traditional and Deep Learning Approaches
The history of the development of the methods of detecting fraud has witnessed
the transition from rule-based systems, then statistical methods, and finally the
emergence of deep learning techniques. In the following paragraphs, the fundamen-
tal deep learning techniques, such as Multi-Layer Perceptrons, Long Short-Term
Memory, and the Transformer, which have influenced the development of the latest
techniques of detecting financial transaction-based fraud, shall be critically evalu-
ated.
2.1.1 Multi-Layer Perceptrons and Feature-Based Modeling
Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) are a basic deep learning architecture for tabular
classification. These models consist of fully connected layers with non-linear acti-
17

=== PDF PAGE 33 ===
vation functions, such as ReLU, and can include dropout for regularization [4]. In
this thesis, the MLP serves as a feature-based baseline for fraud detection using
transaction attributes.
However, MLPs’ rely on the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assump-
tion in which frequently limits their effectiveness. The structural relationships that
define coordinated fraud schemes aren’t considered into account by MLPs since they
regard each transaction as an independent observation [6], [1].
2.1.2 Sequential Modeling with LSTMs and RNNs
In order to overcome the shortcomings of static modeling, Recurrent Neural Net-
works and Long Short-Term Memory networks were introduced to incorporate the
time dependency of user behavior. Moreover, another seminal contribution was
made by Jurgovsky et al. in 2018, in which credit card transactions were mod-
eled as time-series data, and it was shown that sequence classification can enhance
the detection accuracy significantly over traditional machine learning baselines [5].
However, the LSTM model relies on a gate mechanism, consisting of input, forget,
and output gates, which enables the long-term memory of transaction history, which
is essential in identifying anomalies with respect to user behavior [5].
Published research has investigated the use of LSTMs to incorporate transaction
histories into credit-card fraud detection. Jurgovsky et al. evaluated sequence clas-
sification as a way to use information from successive transactions [5]. This provides
a basis for including an LSTM as a sequential baseline in the present comparison.
However, a sequence model operating on an individual entity’s transaction history
18

=== PDF PAGE 34 ===
does not explicitly represent the transaction network. Graph-based approaches pro-
vide a different representation by incorporating connections between transactions
[3].
2.1.3 Transformer Architectures and Self-Attention
The Transformer architecture uses self-attention to learn dependencies between in-
put representations. Unlike recurrent architectures, its attention mechanism does
not require sequential recurrent processing of the input [16].
For tabular data, Gorishniy et al. introduced FT-Transformer, which converts nu-
merical and categorical features into embeddings and applies Transformer layers
to these feature representations [4]. This provides a published example of how self-
attention can be adapted to model interactions between the features of an individual
record.
Such feature-based attention does not explicitly use a transaction graph. Therefore,
tabular Transformers provide a relevant point of comparison with GNNs, which
incorporate graph connectivity into their representations.
2.1.4 Comparative Analysis of Deep Learning Baselines
Feature-based, sequence-based, and graph-based methods use different input repre-
sentations. Their relative performance depends on the dataset, available features,
graph construction, and evaluation protocol. Published results should therefore be
interpreted within their experimental settings rather than as evidence that one model
19

=== PDF PAGE 35 ===
family is universally superior. Published comparative work on Elliptic has reported
benefits from incorporating network information [3]. However, such findings should
be interpreted within the evaluated datasets and experimental settings. They mo-
tivate further comparison rather than establishing that graph-based architectures
invariably outperform non-graph models.
2.2 Foundations of Graph Neural Networks
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) embody a paradigm shift in fraud detection method-
ologies, where the interactions between entities are learned and represented in the
form of a graph. This section discusses the basic architectures of GCNs, Graph-
SAGE, and GAT, which are the building blocks for modern relational learning in
finance.
2.2.1 Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN)
Kipf and Welling (2017) introduced the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) as a
method for semi-supervised classification on graph-structured data [6]. Additionally,
the GCN generalizes the notion of convolution on regular structures such as grids
(images) to more irregular structures using a first-order approximation of spectral
filterings on graphs [6]. However, the underlying mechanism is based on a message
passing framework in which node representations are updated based on aggregations
from their immediate neighbors. Mathematically, the update rule for a GCN layer

2.2.2 GraphSAGE: Inductive Representation Learning
In order to overcome the scalability and transductive restrictions of GCNs, Hamilton
et al. (2017) proposed a new framework called GraphSAGE (Graph Sample and
Aggregate) [7]. In addition to that, GraphSAGE is an inductive framework that
learns a set of aggregator functions instead of a set of node embeddings. This enables
the framework to generate representations for new nodes that have not been seen
during training by sampling and aggregating features over local node neighborhoods
[7]. In addition to that, it incorporates different types of aggregator functions such
as Mean, Pooling, and LSTM [7].
This is especially due to the inductive nature of the GraphSAGE approach. In the

added to the system continuously, the inductive nature of the approach is especially
useful. In particular, the approach is useful for the identification of fraudulent
patterns without having to retrain the model [1], [11]. Moreover, the approach is
also shown to be superior to the GCN approach in the case of large-scale graphs due
to the efficient neighborhood sampling strategy of the approach [1], [3].

Published research has also combined graph modeling with temporal information

2.2.4 Comparative Analysis of GNN Architectures
A comparison of these basic GNN architecture designs shows that they have different
trade-offs. For example, GCN has a robust spectral approach but faces scalability
problems on large graphs. In contrast, GraphSAGE has inductive properties and ef-
ficiency through neighborhood sampling; thus, it is more suitable for industrial-scale
use cases [7], [3]. Another example is GAT, which has the highest expressiveness
because it can learn weights on edges; however, it has a higher computational cost
because of the use of attention in its design [8], [17]. In conclusion, which architec-
ture to use depends on various properties of the dataset that are used in the study,
including graph density, node addition frequency, and complexity of relational fraud
patterns [3].
2.3 GNNs in Financial Fraud Detection: State-
of-the-Art (2023–2026)
In the time period from 2023 to 2026, a new wave of GNN-based innovation has
been designed to specifically tackle the complex needs of financial fraud cases.
2.3.1 Temporal and Heterogeneous Graph Modeling
Financial transactions contain temporal information as well as relationships between
entities. Temporal graph modeling seeks to incorporate this information into the
learned representations. For example, Tian and Liu proposed STA-GT, which in-
23

=== PDF PAGE 39 ===
corporates temporal encoding into a heterogeneous graph neural network and uses
a Transformer module to capture local and global information [10]. This provides a
published example of combining temporal and relational information for transaction
fraud detection.
2.3.2 Hybrid GNN-Transformer Architectures
Another notable trend is the integration of graph-based relational modeling with
Transformer attention. For example, Tian and Liu proposed the Spatial-Temporal-
Aware Graph Transformer (STA-GT) for transaction fraud detection [10]. The
model incorporates temporal encoding into a heterogeneous graph neural network
and uses a Transformer module to capture local and global information through
interactions between nodes.
This approach illustrates how graph structure, temporal information, and attention
can be combined within a fraud detection architecture. It is discussed here as
related work; the present thesis benchmarks GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT against
non-graph deep learning baselines rather than implementing STA-GT.
2.3.3 Self-Supervised and Contrastive Representation Learn-
ing
In order to mitigate the problem of scarcity in labeled data related to fraud, recent
studies have focused on the use of self-supervised or contrastive learning. In this
regard, GNNs are trained to identify ”similar” and ”dissimilar” node pairs in the
24

=== PDF PAGE 40 ===
embedding space, independent of labeled data related to fraud [9], [14]. In other
words, the GNNs are trained on contrastive objectives such as InfoNCE or Triplet
Loss, which enables the models to automatically acquire discriminative representa-
tions for anomaly detection. In addition, recent studies have also demonstrated that
dual-view contrastive learning, which leverages the node local structure and graph
global structure, can enhance the recall of the imbalanced data set [14].
2.4 Comparative Studies on Benchmark Datasets
(IEEE-CIS & Elliptic)
The evaluation of GNNs and deep learning models on standardized benchmarks like
IEEE-CIS and Elliptic provides critical insights into their relative performance.
2.4.1 The Elliptic Bitcoin Dataset
The Elliptic Bitcoin dataset is a benchmark for illicit transaction classification. De-
prez et al. compared network analytics approaches on this dataset, including engi-
neered network features, random-walk methods, and GNNs, and reported improved
predictive performance from network information in their experimental setting [3].
Pareja et al. also evaluated static and dynamic graph-based methods on Ellip-
tic, demonstrating the relevance of temporal modeling to transaction classification
[2]. These studies motivate the present comparison while showing that conclusions
should be tied to the models and evaluation settings examined.

=== PDF PAGE 41 ===
2.4.2 The IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection Dataset
On the other hand, the IEEE-CIS dataset is primarily tabular, so applying GNNs
requires the construction of a graph from transaction attributes. The choice of
relationships used to connect transactions may influence the information available for
neighborhood aggregation. This motivates the comparison of feature-based models
with GNNs and the evaluation of alternative graph constructions in this thesis.
2.5 Handling Extreme Class Imbalance in Graphs
A major methodological challenge faced in fraud detection systems is class imbal-
ance, where fraudulent transactions are substantially outnumbered by legitimate
transactions [11], [15].
2.5.1 Foundations of Imbalance in Fraud Detection
The work of Dal Pozzolo et al. in the year 2015 has provided the basic understanding
of class imbalance in the context of credit card fraud detection using GNNs [19].
The authors of this work have emphasized the effect of extreme skewness on the
calibration of the model and the use of Recall and PR-AUC as opposed to accuracy
in the context of class imbalance [19]. When class imbalance is considered in the
context of GNNs, there is a phenomenon of ”message imbalance” where the features
of a rare fraudulent node are overwhelmed by the features of thousands of benign
nodes [14], [15].
26

=== PDF PAGE 42 ===
2.5.2 Advanced Imbalance Mitigation Techniques
To overcome this issue, recent GNN research has proposed the following graph-aware
strategies:
•Graph-Aware Sampling:Models like PC-GNN (Pick and Choose GNN)
utilize label-balanced sub-samplers to ensure that fraudulent nodes are well-
represented in each training batch [1], [11].
•Weighted Loss Functions:Cost-sensitive learning assigns different im-
portance to errors involving different classes [12]. In weighted binary cross-
entropy, a larger positive-class weight increases the contribution of fraudulent
examples to the training loss, helping to address their underrepresentation.
•Rule Augmentation (REFUEL):The REFUEL framework extracts sym-
bolic rules from the set of fraudulent instances and appends these as node
features, effectively providing a “guided” signal for the GNN model, which is
useful in light of scarce data [13].
•Dual-View Representation Learning:This method employs a pair of
encoders, which attempt to learn the “local” and “global” views of a node,
effectively filtering the minority class signal from the noise of the majority
class [14].
The methods are reviewed as suggested approaches in the literature. In the present
experiments, a binary cross-entropy loss with positive class weighting and validation-
based threshold decision are used; none of the other imbalanced data mitigation
methods that have been discussed are employed or evaluated.

2.6 Identification of Research Gaps
Significant advancements have been made in the area of GNN-based fraud detection,
and yet, several critical gaps in the research area still persist:
•Systematic Comparative Evaluation:The number of papers that com-
pare the performance of recent GNN variants (GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT)
against recent deep learning variants (Transformer, LSTM, MLP) on the same
datasets, after the same preprocessing, is limited. It is difficult to generalize,
as most of the recent work only considers a few variants [3].
•Graph Construction for Tabular Data:While methods exist for con-
structing graphs from tabular data (as in IEEE-CIS), there is no consensus on
the ”optimal” strategy. The effect of edge definition and graph topology on
28

=== PDF PAGE 44 ===
the performance of GNNs has not been sufficiently investigated [3].
•Real-Time Scalability and Latency:Some of the recent advanced GNNs,
such as GAT using temporal attention, are computationally expensive. The
balance between detection accuracy and latency in a production setting is an
area that is a major need in the future research space [9], [17].
•Robustness to Adversarial Camouflage:A major challenge is that fraud-
sters are increasingly trying to ”camouflage” their activities by creating edges
to legitimate nodes. Developing GNNs that are robust to such heterophilic or
adversarial graph structures is a major need in the future work space [1], [15].
In addition to the aforementioned issues, a fundamental modeling limitation of the
existing approaches has also been identified. While the sequential models focus on
the temporal dynamics of the data but fail to capture the relationships between the
entities, the graph-based models are able to capture the relationships between the
entities but often lack the capability to capture the temporal behavioral patterns.
Moreover, most of the research have not focused specifically on the probability cal-
ibration for extreme class imbalance, which is a critical aspect of financial systems’
risk-sensitive decisions.
This is because these constraints highlight the need to have a unified framework,
which integrates time and relational modeling, and produces calibrated probability
values, which can be deployed at scale.
Moreover, in the real world, fraud detection systems have to handle a lot of transac-
tions in real-time and have severe latency constraints. This makes the problem more
29

=== PDF PAGE 45 ===
challenging in terms of scalability and deployment, especially when the problem is
computationally expensive, like in the case of the graph-based model. Finally, the
problem is made more complex because of the need to integrate financial institu-
tions and the evolving nature of fraud, which again makes the need for effective AI
solutions more important.
The above-stated gaps are evaluated and analyzed comparatively using the graph-
construction technique within a single evaluation setup. Scalability, adversarial
robustness, and probability calibration are some of the possible research directions
that could be followed in the future, but not the conclusions of the present study.
2.6.1 Positioning of the Present Study
In this thesis, there is a comparative benchmarking study along with a study on the
sensitivity of construction of graph. The novelty of the research comes from empirical
study on well-known neural networks in different transaction representations. The
difference with other works arises in the research aim and the scope of experiment,
as well as relationship between the models.
In their systematic review of GNN methodologies for financial fraud detection, Motie
and Raahemi categorize the relevant literature according to graph representations,
learning techniques, and application areas [20]. In that respect, their paper presents
a consolidation of prior work, including important research gaps. The current thesis
adds to this perspective in that it conducts experimental analyses comparing GCN,
GraphSAGE, and GAT with more traditional methods including MLP, LSTM, and
Transformer models, using IEEE-CIS and Elliptic data sets. Thus, the contribution

Proposed Approach
3.1 Research Design and Overview
The principal aim and objective of this particular research is to carry out a com-
prehensive and rigorous comparative analysis in relation to Graph Neural Networks
(GNNs) and sophisticated deep learning techniques for the detection of financial
fraud. The overall framework and design of the research are based on a controlled
experimental framework, wherein both relational and feature-based approaches are
subject to comparative analysis under identical and comparable circumstances. The
entire framework is comprised of four principal phases: data characterization, rela-
tional modeling, model implementation, and performance evaluation under extreme
class imbalance.
This methodology assesses the impact of utilizing graph transactions on the effective-
ness of fraud detection when compared to the baselines without graphs. It explores
34

=== PDF PAGE 50 ===
the difference in results obtained from different data sets, architecture, and con-
struction of graphs. The underlying aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness
of the proposed approach by comparing the performance of Graph Convolutional
Networks (GCNs), GraphSAGE, and Graph Attention Networks (GAT) with the
performance of high-capacity deep learning models such as MLP, LSTM, and the
Transformer architecture, with the aim of evaluating the effectiveness of relational
learning [17], [14]. The experimental pipeline has been implemented using a unified
stack based on Python 3.10 and PyTorch with PyTorch Geometric for consistent
and reproducible experiments on the IEEE-CIS and Elliptic datasets.
3.2 Data Sources and Characterization
In this research, two benchmark datasets with unique paradigms in modeling fi-
nancial fraud are utilized, namely, the IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection dataset, which
is tabular-centric, and the Elliptic Bitcoin dataset, which is graph-centric. These
datasets provide a comprehensive basis to test and compare model performance in
a variety of structural settings.

The IEEE-CIS dataset, that’s given by the Vesta Corporation, is one of the most

is spaced at an interval of about two weeks. In each of the steps, there exist
transactions for less than three hours.
•Labels:The nodes can be classified into ’licit’, ’illicit’ and ’unknown’.
3.3 Data Preprocessing and Feature Engineering
In order to maintain a fair comparison, the same preprocessing steps are applied to
the two datasets. This includes the following:
Missing Value Imputation:Features in the IEEE-CIS dataset that have over
80% missing entries were removed to maintain data quality. The Elliptic Bitcoin
dataset was treated by imputing medians into numerical fields and labeling missing
categorical attributes as ‘Unknown’ to ensure the models could still process those
records.
Categorical Encoding:Categorical features were converted into numerical repre-
sentations using label encoding before being supplied to the models.
Numerical Scaling:The numerical input variables were standardized by calculat-
ing the mean and standard deviation from the training data set. This transformation
was then applied to both the validation and test data sets.
Temporal Splitting:To avoid data leakage and better reflect how the model would
37

=== PDF PAGE 53 ===
behave in a real-world setting, the datasets were split based on time instead of using
random sampling. For the IEEE-CIS data, the earliest 80% of transactions were
used for training, the next 10% for validation, and the remaining 10% for testing.
The Elliptic dataset was split chronologically using its time-step information, so that
evaluation used transactions occurring later than those used for training. Temporal
evaluation on Elliptic is also used by Pareja et al. [2].
The same chronological partitions were used across the ten runs and across graph
constructions. Random seeds controlled stochastic aspects of model training rather
than the assignment of transactions to the data partitions.
3.4 Relational Modeling: Graph Construction from
Tabular Data
A key part of this study is converting the tabular IEEE-CIS dataset into a relational
graph format so that Graph Neural Network (GNN) models can be applied. In this
setup, each transaction is treated as a node, while connections between nodes (edges)
are created based on shared transaction features or attributes.
3.4.1 Main Transaction Graph Construction
Each IEEE-CIS transaction is represented as a node. The main construction exam-
ines 14 fields independently:card1–card6,addr1,addr2,P emaildomain,R emaildomain,
DeviceInfo,id 30,id 31, andid 33.
38

=== PDF PAGE 54 ===
For each field, transactions are grouped by equal nonmissing values. Transactions
within each retained group are sorted byTransactionDT, and only consecutive
transactions are connected. The connections obtained from all fields are combined,
with duplicate relationships removed. Matching any one field can therefore establish
an eligible relationship, but not every pair sharing that value is connected.
In case of all five IEEE-CIS structures, the maximum value of matching group size
was fixed at 1,000 transactions. Matching groups greater than 1,000 transactions in
size were not considered for edge creation, but those consisting of 1,000 transactions
were still considered. The transactions falling into the excluded groups were not
discarded from the node set.
The resulting graph contains transaction nodes connected by shared attributes. It
does not introduce separate nodes for cards, addresses, devices, or email domains.
3.4.2 Alternative IEEE-CIS Graph Constructions
Four more approaches to graph construction were explored to see how the definition
and selection of transaction relationships affect the performance of graph neural
networks (GNNs). All approaches keep the same set of transaction nodes and input
features but use different sets of edges.
•Card Construction:Transactions are grouped together based on their joint
signature built fromcard1–card6. The full signature consists of all six values,
including the pattern of missing values. The transactions that have all six
features missing do not create any edges. For partially observable signatures,
39

=== PDF PAGE 55 ===
missing features are marked by a special sentinel. In each selected group,
transactions are sorted byTransactionDT, and edges are created only for
consecutive transactions.
•Address Construction:Transactions are grouped together based on their
joint signature built fromaddr1andaddr2. The transactions that have both
fields missing do not create any edges, while partially observable signatures
are processed the same way as in card construction. Edges are created only
for consecutive transactions.
•Email-Domain Construction:P emaildomainandR emaildomainare pro-
cessed separately. For each of these variables, transactions are grouped into
equal, non-missing values. Each group is sorted on the basis ofTransactionDT,
and connections are made between transactions only in case the latter are con-
secutive. The resulting set of edges is concatenated and duplicates removed.
There is no need for a match between two fields, and missing values result in
no connections.
•Identifier-Time-Window Construction:DeviceInfo,id 30,id 31, and
id 33are processed separately. In each of these, transactions are first grouped
on the basis of identical non-missing values. After the application of the group-
size condition, all unique transaction pairs where the difference between the
timestamp values is less than or equal to 86,400 seconds are connected, even
those that have 24 hours of difference between their timestamp values.
All constructions reject matches of groups larger than the pre-defined maximum
group size. All transactions that are not included in a given group will be nodes in
40

=== PDF PAGE 56 ===
the graph and may get connections through some other valid group, if any.
All constructions differ in terms of matching conditions and selection of edges. The
card and address constructions use a joint signature, while the email-domain and
identifier constructions work on each field separately. In addition, the former three
constructions link consecutive transactions, while the temporal construction links
all valid pairs in its window.

3.4.5 Graph Access Across Data Partitions
During GNN learning, the graph includes training, validation, and test nodes, and
the supervised loss is only computed on training nodes. Labels for validation and
test nodes are not included in gradient-based optimization, although their node
features and connectivity might be involved in the message-passing process. The
aforementioned scenario is referred to as transductive evaluation. Therefore, tem-
porally defined label splits do not imply that training representations depend only
on data prior to the validation or test stages.
Relations are stored bidirectionally. This implies that temporally defined edge selec-
tion rules define which transactions can be connected but not that message passing
should be restricted to the past.

3.7 Class Imbalance Mitigation Strategies
In cases where there are very few instances of fraud compared to legitimate transac-
tions, specific methods for learning have to be employed to ensure that the algorithm
does not simply converge into predicting everything as legitimate [11], [19].

To address class imbalance, all models are trained using binary cross-entropy with
logits and positive-class weighting. The positive-class weight is defined as

3.9 Evaluation Framework and Metrics
In the presence of extreme class imbalance, traditional accuracy is a misleading
metric. This study employs a multi-faceted evaluation framework focusing on the
minority class.
•Recall (Detection Rate):Calculates the percentage of real fraudulent
transactions that the model properly detects. For financial organizations look-
ing to reduce losses, this is the most important statistic [11], [19].
•F1-Score:A balanced indicator of the model’s capacity to detect fraud with-
out producing an excessive number of false alarms, it is the harmonic mean of
precision and recall [17], [13].
•Precision-Recall AUC (PR-AUC):In contrast to ROC-AUC, PR-AUC
offers a more accurate evaluation in highly skewed datasets and is sensitive to
the minority class’s performance [15], [19].
•Precision:The proportion of transactions predicted as fraudulent that are
truly fraudulent.
•False Positives:The number of legitimate test transactions classified as
fraudulent at the threshold selected using validation F1-score.

•ROC-AUC:A complementary measure of discrimination across classifica-
tion thresholds, based on the relationship between the true-positive and false-
positive rates.

Chapter Four
Results
4.1 Introduction
This chapter presents the experimental outcomes of the fraud detection models
built in this research work. The main objective of carrying out the experiments is
to compare classical models against deep learning models with GNN for financial
fraud detection in class-imbalanced situations.
In this regard, two benchmark datasets have been used: IEEE-CIS Fraud Detec-
tion Dataset and Elliptic Bitcoin Dataset. The IEEE-CIS dataset represents the
transaction-based fraud detection setting while the Elliptic dataset represents the
graph-based fraud detection setting.
The considered models are divided into two types of models. The first type includes
non-graph deep learning models: MLP, LSTM, and Transformer. The second type,
on the other hand, includes graph neural network models: GCN, GraphSAGE, and
50

=== PDF PAGE 66 ===
Graph Attention Network (GAT).
To assess their performance, recall, precision, F1-score, PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, and
confusion matrix values are used to evaluate the models. Since the task of fraud
detection is an example of a highly unbalanced classification problem, PR-AUC and
F1-score are consequently regarded as the most important metrics of evaluation. In
addition, recall is also analyzed carefully since this metric reflects the model’s ability
to detect the fraudulent transactions. Nevertheless, recall was not interpreted in
isolation, as a high recall value can similarly be achieved by a model that considers
many legitimate transactions to be fraudulent ones.
Furthermore, in order to make the comparison more reliable, the main experiment is
repeated using 10 pairs of random seeds. Following this, the Wilcoxon signed-rank
test is performed on the pairs’ results.
4.2 Experimental Setup
All the experiments have been done in Python using PyTorch and PyTorch Geo-
metric. Specifically, PyTorch was used to implement MLP, LSTM, and Transformer
models while PyTorch Geometric was used to implement graph-based models like
GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT.
For the IEEE-CIS dataset, the transactions and identities tables have been concate-
nated on the TransactionID column. Subsequently, features with more than 80%
missing values have been dropped. Regarding the remaining data, label encoding
has been performed for categorical features, whereas missing values for numerical
51

=== PDF PAGE 67 ===
features have been imputed using the median and scaled for standardizing the data.
Finally, a chronological split has been done such that the earliest 80% of transac-
tions are in the training set, the next 10% in the validation set, and the last 10% in
the test set.
In contrast, for the Elliptic dataset, the features of the nodes, class labels, and
edge list of transactions are loaded initially. Next, labels have been transformed
to binary form where 1 denotes illicit transactions and 0 denotes licit transactions.
Meanwhile, nodes with unknown labels are not included in the supervised training,
validation, and testing masks. Instead, the nodes with known labels are sorted based
on their time step and, in a similar chronological fashion, split into 70% training,
15% validation, and 15% test sets.
Taining of all models was done using BCEWithLogitsLoss with weighting on the
positive class due to imbalanced classes. To optimize the parameters, the Adam
optimizer was used with a learning rate of 0.001 and weight decay of 1e-5. Furether-
more, early stopping was used with the validation PR-AUC metric as a monitor. All
models except GNN were trained with a maximum number of epochs equal to 50
and a patience of 10, whereas the GNN models were trained with up to 100 epochs
and a patience of 15.
Following the training phase, the best checkpoint was selected based on validation
PR-AUC. Subsequently, the classification threshold was fine-tuned on the validation
dataset by optimizing the validation F1-score.

4.8 Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Statistical Testing
In order to determine the statistical significance of the detected difference between
the models, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to the paired experiments.
Such an approach was chosen primarily because of its non-parametric character and
its corresponding ability to compare paired data without making any assumptions
regarding the normal distribution of the score differences.
To facilitate this, equal random seeds were used for each model pair, thereby pro-
ducing paired scores. Under this framework, the Null Hypothesis states that there is
no difference between the two models, evaluated against a chosen significance level
ofα= 0.05.
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied to the main-model and alternative graph-
construction experiments with ten paired runs. Sampled-complement configurations
were excluded because only one run was available for each configuration. The re-
ported p-values are unadjusted for multiple comparisons and are interpreted at a
nominal significance level of 0.05.

4.9 Discussion
The results are explained in terms of the settings when graph-based modeling is ef-
fective, how much other graph representations can improve a Graph Neural Network
71

=== PDF PAGE 87 ===
(GNN), and in what cases non-graph-based models still outperform. Outperforming
another representation suggests dependence on the particular relations, but it does
not mean superiority over non-graph-based models per se. Thus, the distinction
between the model comparison and the construction comparison is made, and what
graph properties are linked with the results is discussed.
In the context of the IEEE-CIS dataset, the MLP, LSTM, and Transformer models
demonstrated better mean PR-AUC and F1-scores than GNN models. However, in
these models, the MLP had the best mean PR-AUC score, and the Transformer had
the best mean F1-score. However, the differences between PR-AUC scores of these
three models were not statistically significant. The possible cause of the models’
performance is related to the intrinsic nature of the dataset, which is tabular in
its nature with transactional features that have information. While there was a
construction of the graph by means of shared identifiers, it was very sparse and
fragmented, having 255,381 connected components.
In addition, the results of the IEEE-CIS experiment showed that creating a graph
out of tabular data is not always beneficial. In particular, shared identifiers may
connect transactions whose relationships are not informative for fraud detection,
potentially introducing noise into neighborhood aggregation.
GraphSAGE: email-domain connections.The email-domain graph shows the
highest mean PR-AUC and F1-score of GraphSAGE constructions. Edges link ad-
jacent transactions in each group that have the same value in either email-domain
column, processing each column separately. Groups with more than 1,000 transac-
tions are not included in generating edges but still contribute to the list of nodes.
72

=== PDF PAGE 88 ===
Transactions with large sets are ignored. In this way, a highly limited set of rela-
tionships is generated where 97.2689% of all transactions have no relationship and
2.54% of fraud transactions have any neighbor. Despite this, the GraphSAGE con-
struction on this graph performs better than the one for the main graph in PR-AUC,
F1-score, recall, and mean false positive count.
It might be due to the fact that limiting the set of relationships to this limited
set reduces the effect of less informative neighbors which were added by using the
larger main graph. Nevertheless, the results do not show that the relationships of
the email-domain graph are in general more informative than others. In this con-
struction, most transactions get no information from other transactions. Thus, the
observed improvement might be related to changes in the set of connected transac-
tions, learned parameters shared by all nodes, or both.
GCN: identifier connections within 24 hours.The 24-hour identifier graph
produces the maximum mean PR-AUC and F1-scores for GCN. This construct links
all pairs of transactions that qualify and have a common non-missing value in at
least one of the selected identifiers, given that their time stamps are within 24 hours
of each other. Links are not limited to sequential transactions. This graph has the
maximum number of fraud-fraud edges equal to 16,584. But, 92.9842% of the total
nodes are isolated, meaning that its edges have been generated from a small portion
of transactions.
This can be due to the fact that the neighborhoods generated based on the temporal
constraint and pair selection rule could be more beneficial for GCN aggregation than
neighborhoods created using the main graph. This is still a hypothesis since the
73

=== PDF PAGE 89 ===
construction makes modifications to the selected attributes, temporal constraint,
and pair selection rule. In addition, the increased mean PR-AUC score is also
associated with an increase in false positives and variations between different runs.
GAT: the broader main construction.The best average PR-AUC and F1-
Score is attained by GAT using the main graph. This graph is based on consecutive
transaction connections that were derived separately from fourteen features. It has
the least isolation rate of 42.5837% and associates the most fraudulent transactions
with a neighbor, with 65.67%.
The greater number of connections may be the potential explanation of the higher
performance of GAT using the main graph as construction: limitation of relations
may eliminate important information for the model. Yet, there is no evidence that
the attention mechanism of GAT manages to select important connections or that
the higher number of connections leads to the better result.
Implications for graph construction.However, no construction performs best
on all three architectures. Specifically, the email-domain graph yields the highest
GraphSAGE mean PR-AUC but the lowest GAT mean PR-AUC. In a similar vein,
while the wider fraud-node coverage of the main graph does not make it the best
construction for GraphSAGE and GCN, density, isolation, and fraud-fraud edge
numbers cannot independently explain the ranking of the constructions.
It is also important to take into consideration how the construction rules influence
the interpretation of the statistics. First, the consecutive-transaction linkage limits
the number of edges that could exist between nodes in eligible groups, while the
74

=== PDF PAGE 90 ===
identifier-time-window construction allows for many edges to appear within a small
group of transactions. The elimination of oversized groups will further limit the
coverage of transactions.
These architectural dependencies are shown through the structural measures, which
apply to the whole graph, and the performance measures, which apply to the test set.
Individual testing of connected and isolated test transactions as well as individual
modifications of individual construction rules would have to be used in order to
further investigate the suggested explanations.
In the Elliptic dataset, GraphSAGE provided higher average values of PR-AUC
and F1-score in comparison with the MLP; yet, there was no statistical difference
according to the performed tests between the two algorithms. Transformer and
LSTM performed better on these two indicators. Thus, the presence of a graph in the
data set does not provide an advantage for the studied GNNs. One possible reason
could be that the node features in Elliptic include already aggregated transactions,
which can be used by non-graph algorithms as well.
Similarly, the results show the need for analysis of several different measures of
performance. Some models showed high recall and low precision. For example,
the GAT algorithm used on the IEEE-CIS dataset achieved the highest recall rate
in all the six main models in the final run, finding 940 fraudulent transactions
but generating 3,888 false alarms. In comparison, the LSTM found 879 fraudulent
transactions with 854 false alarms, which resulted in much higher F1-score. In
real-world fraud detection systems, too many false alarms generate expenses due
to increased work for fraud specialists and potential damage to the reputation of
75

=== PDF PAGE 91 ===
legitimate clients.
Therefore, PR-AUC and F1-score provide a better overall evaluation under class
imbalance. Recall remains important, but it should be interpreted together with
precision and false-positive counts.
Despite the fact that the sampled-complement experiment failed to improve the
prediction quality consistently and often led to worse PR-AUC values, it replaced the
existing associations with the sampled non-edges while at the same time increasing
the level of connectivity. In other words, the changes that were noticed can not be
explained merely by the graph density.
4.10 Answering the Research Questions
RQ1: Can Graph Neural Networks (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT) outper-
form advanced deep learning architectures (MLP, LSTM, Transformer-
based models) by learning network-level dependencies within financial
transaction data?
The findings show no conclusive evidence of the superiority of graph neural net-
works (GNNs) compared to the non-graph models that were tested. The case with
IEEE-CIS is that the non-graph models had a higher mean PR-AUC and F1-score
compared to all the graph neural networks considered. However, there was no sta-
tistically significant difference in the PR-AUC of the MLP and GraphSAGE with
email-domain links (unadjusted Wilcoxon test, p = 0.0645). In that respect, the
two models cannot be concluded to be equivalent.
76

=== PDF PAGE 92 ===
For Elliptic, GraphSAGE achieved the highest mean PR-AUC and F1-score among
the evaluated GNNs, while Transformer and LSTM achieved higher values on both
metrics.
RQ2: Under which dataset and graph-construction conditions do the
evaluated GNNs provide useful fraud detection performance, and how
are their performance differences associated with graph connectivity, iso-
lation, and class-specific relationships?
The value that could be derived from graph-based models depended on the architec-
ture of the model, the way it was constructed, and the comparison point selected. On
IEEE-CIS, For the IEEE-CIS dataset, GraphSAGE exhibited higher performance
for the email domain graph than the general graph: PR-AUC was improved from
0.3432 to 0.3912, F1-score from 0.3788 to 0.4025, and false positives from 1,561.4 to
1,391.4. On the other hand, GCN achieved its best mean PR-AUC in the 24-hour
identifier graph, whereas GAT showed its maximum mean PR-AUC in the main
graph.
The gains achieved by the graph-specific improvements did not result in an advan-
tage over the non-graph approaches. The mean PR-AUC and F1 scores for all eval-
uated IEEE-CIS GNN architectures were consistently below those for MLP, LSTM,
and Transformer networks. The PR-AUC comparison between MLP and Graph-
SAGE for the email domain links was not statistically significant in the provided
unadjusted test (p= 0.0645). This outcome does not prove the equivalence of the
models. In case of Elliptic data, the GraphSAGE outperformed MLP in terms of the
mean PR-AUC and F1-score but lagged behind Transformer and LSTM networks.
77

=== PDF PAGE 93 ===
No single property that explained the success or failure of any architectural form
could be determined from the structural analysis. Increased connectivity and fraud
node connectivity did not necessarily reflect good performance, while the strongest
version of GraphSAGE was relatively sparse. As a result, the findings show that
graph architecture construction should be evaluated on an architecture-specific ba-
sis. The connections between the provided relations and performance have been
established, not causations of graph properties.
RQ3: Which modeling paradigm achieves superior fraud detection per-
formance, particularly in terms of Recall, F1-score, and PR-AUC under
highly imbalanced fraud detection conditions?
Non-graph models showed better performance according to mean PR-AUC and F1
score. In the case of IEEE-CIS data, MLP showed the best mean PR-AUC and
precision, while Transformer showed the best mean F1 score. In the case of principal
and alternative graph structures, GraphSAGE with address-based edges exhibited
the best mean recall value of 0.4175. Differences of PR-AUC values between MLP,
LSTM, and Transformer were not statistically significant. In the case of the Elliptic
dataset, the Transformer model performed the best in mean PR-AUC, F1 score, and
precision.
GNN models sometimes achieved competitive recall. In particular, GCN achieved
the highest mean recall on Elliptic, but its low precision resulted in a lower F1-
score. Therefore, recall alone did not identify the strongest overall model. Under
the class imbalance conditions and experimental settings of this study, non-graph
deep learning provided the strongest overall results on the primary metrics of PR-

AUC and F1-score.

Chapter Five
Conclusion and Future Work
5.1 Conclusion
In this thesis, the situations where existing graph neural networks perform well in
fraud detection are evaluated along with the situations where the non-graph neural
networks continue to outperform. This work includes benchmarking of IEEE-CIS
and Elliptic datasets along with the investigation into the effect of transaction graph
generation on the performance of GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT.
The two datasets provide separate evaluation settings: IEEE-CIS requires the de-
velopment of relations using attributes from tables, while Elliptic offers transactions
graph. In IEEE-CIS, there are five constructions that allow evaluating graph neu-
ral networks architectures based on different definitions of relations between trans-
actions. The comparisons performed show the strengths and weaknesses of each
construction separately, and statistics about structure help to interpret the results
80

=== PDF PAGE 96 ===
obtained. Neither the cross-dataset evaluation nor the construction experiments
separate the effect of one graph property on performance.
In total, six models were considered. On one hand, non-graph models consist of
the MLP, LSTM, and Transformer, whereas the graph models include the GCN,
GraphSAGE, and GAT. In terms of evaluation, recall, precision, F1-score, PR-AUC,
ROC-AUC, and confusion matrices were applied. Moreover, taking into account the
fact that there was significant class imbalance in fraud detection, emphasis was put
on PR-AUC and F1-score as primary metrics, since accuracy has shown misleading
results in such cases according to previous research [15], [19].
From the results, it can be noted that there was no clear indication that the graph-
based models outperformed the non-graph models across the board. In the case
of the IEEE-CIS data set, MLP had the highest average PR-AUC value, while the
Transformer had the highest average F1-score value. It was also found that the pro-
cess of constructing the graph depends on the model used. For example, in the case
of GraphSAGE, the best graph construction would be the one that involves email
domain connectivity, while in the case of Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), the
best graph construction is the 24-hour identifier construction.
Conversely, on the Elliptic dataset, the best results in terms of overall performance
are achieved by the Transformer network, followed by the LSTM algorithm. As
for the GNNs, GraphSAGE turned out to be the best performing architecture and
showed higher competitiveness in comparison to the IEEE-CIS case. This result is
compatible with the potential usefulness of network information discussed in pub-
lished Elliptic research [3], although the present comparison does not isolate graph
81

=== PDF PAGE 97 ===
structure as the cause of the observed performance differences. However, even in
the case of the Elliptic dataset, the best non-graph models still outperformed the
GNN models in terms of PR-AUC and F1-score.
The statistical tests further confirmed these findings. On IEEE-CIS, MLP achieved
the highest mean PR-AUC, while Transformer achieved the highest mean F1-score.
The reported tests did not establish significant PR-AUC differences among the three
non-graph models. Among the GNN configurations, GraphSAGE with email-domain
connections achieved the strongest mean PR-AUC, and its PR-AUC difference from
MLP was not statistically significant in the reported unadjusted test. Similarly,
for the Elliptic dataset, the Transformer algorithm significantly outperformed the
competing algorithms, while GraphSAGE turned out to be the best performing GNN
architecture. Thus, it can be said that the effectiveness of the models depends not
only on the possibility to represent the data as a graph but also on the quality of
the graph, the informativeness of the node features, and the class imbalance level.
These results show that graph building should be considered together with the struc-
ture of the model itself and not just assume that using graph-based models increases
the effectiveness of fraud detection. The findings are specific to the datasets, model
structures, and methodology used in this thesis.
The sequence model experiment results use the first nine labeled instances of each
fold since they cannot have incomplete windows of inputs. There is an inherent
evaluation disparity with respect to other models in addition to a potential for bias.
Validation set exclusions might influence choice of checkpointing and thresholds.
There has been no quantification of the influence of this on the rankings/comparisons
82

=== PDF PAGE 98 ===
between models.
There are several limitations that need to be taken into account when consider-
ing these findings. First, the experiment measures two different data sets using
fixed partitions along time axis, so that variability between random seeds can be
attributed mostly to stochastic properties of the training process itself and not to
any differences due to independent sampling of data sets. Statistical tests used in
the experiment are not corrected for multiple comparisons. Furthermore, only one
instance of each sampled-complement configuration was tested. Alternative graphs
differ by a number of variables at once, including attribute matching, pair selection
rule, and group size effects, making it impossible to isolate the effect of each variable
separately.
5.2 Future Work
Some areas for further research could include the following. First, further investiga-
tion of graph construction approaches that are applicable to tabular data, such as
the IEEE-CIS dataset, is possible. In this research, the graph was built on the basis
of common ids; however, other options could be considered as well and provide a
more significant structure. For example, one could use a heterogeneous graph that
represents not only transactions but also such node types as cards, devices, accounts,
and email domains.
Second, the study of temporal graph neural networks is highly possible. Given
that fraud actions have inherent temporal characteristics, and some datasets—for
83

=== PDF PAGE 99 ===
example, IEEE-CIS and Elliptic—have time information, this structural dimension
can be actively used in the process of fraud detection.
Third, further investigation of some imbalance-aware GNN approaches is needed.
Because datasets on fraud detection are significantly imbalanced, standard message-
passing tends to weaken the minority class signal. Some examples of methods that
could subsequently be applied are: graph-aware sampling, focal loss variants, minor-
ity class neighborhood sampling, and dual-view graph representation learning [11],
[14], [15].
Fourth, further study related to the calibration and optimization of the threshold
should be carried out, since the results show that some of the models can achieve
high recall but low precision. In practice, this imbalance means that there are a large
number of fraud detections along with many false positives. Accordingly, probability
calibration and cost-sensitive thresholds based on false positive/negative costs could
be considered for further research [19].
Fifth, further testing of the model on more datasets would allow researchers to
improve the generality of their conclusions. Specifically, testing models on a greater
variety of banking, credit card, cryptocurrency, and anti-money laundering datasets
would help to learn more about the exact circumstances under which the application
of GNNs is beneficial or not compared to feature-based models.
In conclusion, possible areas for future research include hybrid approaches that
incorporate graph-based relational modeling together with temporal modeling or
transformer-based attention mechanisms. The well-known STA-GT model serves
as an excellent starting point in this direction [10]. Further research is needed to
84

determine if such combinations perform better than the models considered in this
thesis.
